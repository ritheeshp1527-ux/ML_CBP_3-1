A B S T R A C T
Credit card transaction fraud is one of the challenging security concerns for financial firms globally. The
continuously changing environment of fraud characteristics and the class imbalance and complete separation
issues in fraud data create difficulties in accurately and efficiently predicting fraudulent transactions and
implementing fraud detection systems in real-time. The study aims to develop a new real-time fraud detection
framework that can efficiently be implemented online and address the issues caused by non-stationary changes
in transaction and fraud characteristics, class imbalance, and complete separation. We propose a new approach
to handle the impact of non-stationary changes in fraud transaction patterns. It enables efficiency in model
training, given the sheer size of datasets. By implementing a robust fuzzy logistic regression model against
class imbalance and separation problems, we address the challenge of having a very low rate of fraudulent
transactions in the dataset and having separation issues due to specific characteristics of transactions. The
analysis of the performance versus efficiency nexus of the proposed methodology reveals that the proposed
framework shows strong performance results with specificity and sensitivity greater than 0.90 and Matthew’s
correlation coefficient greater than 0.80 even on small sample sizes and produces highly accurate results in
identifying fraudulent and non-fraudulent transactions with an accuracy greater than 0.99. Benchmarking with
machine learning and other fraud detection approaches reveals that the proposed framework provides better
detection performance while maintaining a higher rate of identifying non-fraudulent transactions than the
alternative approaches. Improved classification performance leads to detecting fraudulent transactions with
higher precision while avoiding misclassifying legitimate transfers, resulting in lower financial losses and
improved customer satisfaction.
1. Introduction
Credit card fraud poses a significant financial burden on the global
economy. 3.5 billion fraudulent card transactions were recorded in
2022 globally, up from just 1.5 billion in 1992, which resulted in
a total loss of 321 billion US dollars ( Aite-Novarica, 2022 ; Nilson-
Report, 2024). Furthermore, the frequency of fraud activity has grown
by 133% in the last 30 years. For example, in Australia, 1.7 million
people were victims of fraud activity in 2021 and 2022, which is
equivalent to 8.1% of the country’s total population (ABS, 2023). Credit
card fraud involves the unauthorized use of cards linked to legitimate
consumer accounts. It can be detected as unauthorized transactions on
a customer’s banking accounts, where a transaction is usually flagged
as fraudulent (𝑌 = 1 ) or not (𝑌 = 0 ). In response to the escalation of
fraudulent activities, it is imperative to harness capabilities from data
science methodologies to facilitate precise predictions of such events.
There are three key characteristics of credit card fraud detection
in the literature: Firstly, fraud characteristics are non-stationary and

The core methods to detect fraudulent anomalies in transaction data
include machine learning (ML), statistical, and fuzzy approaches. A
systematic review of literature on credit card fraud detection using
approaches such as support vector machines (SVMs), random forests,
neural networks (NNs), deep learning methods, K-nearest neighbors
(KNN), and extended gradient boosted tree is presented by Ghai and
Kang (2022). Another extensive systematic literature survey by Abdal-
lah, Maarof, and Zainal (2016) provides a comprehensive overview of
issues impacting the performance of fraud detection systems.
Hilal, Gadsden, and Yawney (2022) explore SVMs in detail, propos-
ing seven variations ranging from classical SVMs to incremental clus-
tering, weighted one-class discriminative feature selection, and KNN
models. A classification performance analysis is given by assessing
the overall accuracy of each method applied to machine learning
datasets. Shen, Tong, and Deng (2007) focus on using decision trees
against NNs and classical logistic regression on a credit card transac-
tions dataset, which is not freely available, to compare the performance
of considered methods. Fang, Zhang, and Huang (2019) propose over-
sampling in ML methods to improve classification performance given
the imbalance problem in transaction fraud data. They compare a light
gradient boosting model against random forests and gradient boosting
machines, indicating that the former produces stronger classification
results with better efficiency. Strelcenia and Prakoonwit (2023) explore
various data augmentation methods, such as kernelized conditional
generative adversarial networks. The authors focus on how such meth-
ods perform under the significant imbalance setting for fraud detection,
showcasing strong F1 and accuracy scores. Similarly, Ba (2019) uses
generative adversarial networks (GANs) for fraud detection using real
credit card transaction data from 2015, which is also used in this
study. Chen, Lee, Huang, and Peng (2024) propose using intelligent
sampling with a self-supervised learning algorithm for the binary clas-
sification of two problems involving credit card fraud and card default
rates. The authors explore the use of such machine learning techniques
against imbalance problems and real-time implementation.
The classical statistical approach in fraud detection involves us-
ing logistic regression for the classification task (Ghai & Kang, 2022;
Shen et al., 2007). However, classical logistic regression showcases
a low fraud detection rate with significant inaccuracy, which can
be attributed to the fact that classical logistic regression methods
are vulnerable to imbalance and separation issues, resulting in low
classification accuracy (Charizanos, Demirhan, & İçen, 2023).
Fuzzy methods constitute another class of methods employed for
detecting fraudulent transactions. Lenard and Alam (2005) analyze
using fuzzy logic and fuzzy clustering methods for fraud detection.
The authors explore both the benefits and critical challenges of fuzzy
methods for fraud detection from a theoretical perspective, conclud-
ing that fuzzy methods can offer significant opportunities for fraud
detection for online card transactions. Casalino, Castellano, and Mencar
(2019) consider using fuzzy techniques for fraud detection, such as
dynamic incremental semi-supervised fuzzy c-means clustering. The
application is done on the same credit card transaction dataset as in this
study. The authors conclude that the proposed fuzzy method performs
well in handling the imbalance problem. Behera and Panigrahi (2015)
propose using a fuzzy NN model that involves an input fuzzy rule
combiner component with an NN learning component, which shows
promising results in handling imbalance and classification accuracy.
We compare the model proposed in our study against Behera and
Panigrahi (2015)’s work. However, it is not possible to compare the
performance of our model to Casalino et al. (2019)’s model since they
do not cover the performance measures we consider. Razooqi, Khurana,
Raahemifar, and Abhari (2016) propose a fuzzy logic methodology for
fraud detection, which is compared against artificial neural networks
(ANNs). The authors analyze the fuzzification and de-fuzzification of
input and output information used to predict probabilities in a fuzzy
logic model, then implement this model in five steps. The classification
results, evaluated using only mean squared error, indicate that the ANN
model outperforms the fuzzy method. However, multiple measures are
required to provide targeted insights towards a confusion matrix. Jain,
Tiwari, Dubey, and Jain (2019) explore several algorithms used for
fraud detection, including SVMs, ANNs, KNN, decision trees, and a
fuzzy logic methodology. The authors showcase a confusion matrix
approach to validate classification performance. The ANN model out-
performs all other considered methods using a percentage accuracy
score. However, using a single performance measure does not ensure
generalizability and only provides insights into the model’s overall
accuracy. It does not provide a complete assessment of how these
models perform under imbalance and separation.
Hybrid models are also considered for fraud detection in the lit-
erature. Dal Pozzolo, Boracchi, Caelen, Alippi, and Bontempi (2017)
and Dal Pozzolo et al. (2014) use a two-step method with two semi-
supervised models. One model is implemented online in a smaller
transaction sample size, and the other is used to train the model on
the whole dataset. This involves implementing the method in real-time
using a small sample to train the model, followed by a retrospec-
tive analysis on a much larger dataset to validate model parameter
selection and performance. Srivastava, Kundu, Sural, and Majumdar
(2008) consider using hidden Markov models (HMMs), trained by using
behavioral statistics of cardholders in transaction data. The authors
use a process of probabilistic elimination with the HMM model, where
if a transaction does not produce a sufficiently high probability, it is
classified as fraudulent. Maes, Tuyls, Vanschoenwinkel, and Manderick
(2002) propose using a Bayesian belief network model instead while
they compare against an NN approach. However, the Bayesian and
NN models showcase a low true positive rate, indicating a weak fraud
detection accuracy. HaratiNik, Akrami, Khadivi, and Shajari (2012)
propose a hybrid model that utilizes Fogg behavioral analysis on the
merchants, assessing their motivation and ability towards card fraud.
Information from this analysis is then used to obtain fraud tendency
weights, which predict fraudulent activity, given a set of fuzzy rules.
The model showcases strong true positive rates against standard fuzzy
methods with a moderately high false positive rate. While this could be
attributed to imbalance issues, the authors do not use a comprehensive
set of performance measures to explore the impact of imbalance and
separation.
Critical limitations to these analytical methods encompass several
important aspects. There is a lack of focus on imbalance issues in fraud
detection studies (Behera & Panigrahi, 2015; Ghai & Kang, 2022; Maes
et al., 2002). Transaction data are significantly imbalanced towards
non-fraudulent activities. The presence of a class imbalance in the
data can bias the model to predict mostly the majority class. This
leads to poor performance in detecting fraudulent activity, which is
the minority class in the binary responses. It may also lead to a
model that is less sensitive to detecting the true underlying patterns
and associations in fraudulent transactions, hence more vulnerability
towards false negative predictions. While a few studies for fraud de-
tection mention the imbalance issue (Abdallah et al., 2016; Ba, 2019;
Hilal et al., 2022), these do not explore the details of any potential
impact. Behera and Panigrahi (2015) evaluate a hybrid of a rule-based
fuzzy inference system and NN for fraud detection by utilizing Receiver
Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves to assess classification perfor-
mance. However, due to the lack of consideration of the specificity
and sensitivity, it is unclear how the imbalance issues in the data
influence the proposed model’s classification performance. Ghai and
Kang (2022) explore several different ML methods to propose solutions
to the fraud detection problem. However, there is no consideration of
the class imbalance problem in Ghai and Kang (2022)’s study, which
could be achieved by employing a comprehensive assessment of the
sensitivity and specificity performance measures. Hilal et al. (2022)

extensive analysis of classification performance, providing greater in-
sights into how the model performs in general. However, the exclusion
of sensitivity and specificity measures does not provide an additional
layer of assessment targeted at the imbalance problem. Moreover, the
class imbalance problem is often approached from a theoretical stand-
point with only accuracy and F1 scores being considered (Strelcenia
& Prakoonwit, 2023). However, in summary, this crucial issue needs
to be assessed and discussed thoroughly as part of analyzing the model
results using a comprehensive set of performance measures that include
both sensitivity and specificity scores.
The lack of consideration on the issue of complete separation and its
impact on classification performance is another limitation in the liter-
ature. Separation can occur even on significantly imbalanced datasets.
For example, consider fraudulent activity that could be associated with
higher transaction amounts, specific geographic locations, or specific
consumer demographics, such as age or profession. This could mean
that certain consumer populations are more vulnerable to online fraud;
hence, separation may occur. Even if fraudulent activity is the minority
class with significant imbalance, these characteristics would perfectly
separate the probability of a fraudulent transaction. The presence of
complete separation leads to a model that fits the training data excep-
tionally well but performs poorly with data outside the training set
due to being unable to learn from meaningful patterns in the data.
Therefore, separation should be considered while assessing classifica-
tion performance. However, while Abdallah et al. (2016) and Hilal
et al. (2022) explore the problem of class imbalance to a great extent
from a theoretical perspective, there is no mention of the problem of
complete separation in fraud datasets and how this could influence
classification performance through over-fitting issues. Ba (2019) claims
that the proposed methodology is robust towards imbalance problems,
with strong performance scores across several measures. However,
there is no exploration of how classification performance could be
artificially inflated due to over-fitting caused by separation issues.
Lastly, while Strelcenia and Prakoonwit (2023) provide an extensive
discussion on the imbalance issue, the authors do not explore how
the presence of separation would impact classification performance.
Moreover, several instances could benefit from analyzing the separation
issue and the concept of over-fitting (Behera & Panigrahi, 2015; Shen
et al., 2007).
The reviewed literature showcases a limited number of measures
used to assess the overall performance of the implemented models. Be-
hera and Panigrahi (2015) only utilize the ROC curves to assess classifi-
cation performance, while Shen et al. (2007) consider a cumulative rate
of decline percentage score. On the other hand, Strelcenia and Prakoon-
wit (2023) consider an extensive selection of classification performance
measures, such as precision, recall, F1 score and accuracy. However,
the lack of specificity and sensitivity scores do not allow for a complete
assessment of imbalance, while Mathew’s Correlation Coefficient (MCC)
would have provided further insights towards separation. While Chen
et al. (2024)’s approach showcases strong F1 performance across both
datasets, the set of performance measures is not sufficient to fully assess
the response of proposed algorithms to the imbalance and separation
problems. The authors provide only F1, precision, and recall as mea-
sures, which do not fully showcase that the model responds well to the
imbalance problem. This is due to the lack of analysis on variability
between performance sensitivity and specificity, which would be a
strong indicator of handling imbalance problems. Moreover, the lack
of sufficient performance measures does not allow for a comprehensive
assessment of potential over-fitting caused by complete separation in
the data.
A comprehensive set of measures such as sensitivity, specificity,
MCC, and F1 score provide a more thorough understanding of the
combined influence of class imbalance with complete separation on a
classifier. The presence of perfect scores across specificity, sensitivity,
and F1 scores strongly indicates that a model learns extensively from
a distinct group of predictors, hence not identifying other patterns
successfully. The limited number of performance measures employed
per study in the literature indicates the need for such an assessment.
Moreover, the small set of performance metrics and lack of sensitivity
and specificity assessment make it difficult to provide targeted insights
towards imbalance issues (Charizanos et al., 2023). The studies that do
not consider sensitivity and specificity together potentially overlook the
impact of class imbalance and report a model as promising while it is
severely impacted by separation.
Moreover, there is limited consideration of the non-stationary
changes in fraud characteristics. Fraud transaction data include sev-
eral consumer and merchant characteristics that influence fraudster
and consumer behavior towards credit card transactions. These rapid
changes in behaviors impact how efficiently and accurately a financial
institution can react to fraudulent activity. As such, these charac-
teristics of fraudulent activity can also influence the classification
performance of any given model when applied in real-time. Kur-
shan et al. (2020) focus on the non-stationary characteristics of fraud
through the lenses of the different types of fraudulent activity and how
these influence behavior. They identify and explore the eight most
common types of fraud, ranging from online payments to fraudulent
transactions due to identity theft and elderly scams. It is shown that
different fraud types impact consumers differently based on certain
demographic elements. Furthermore, the authors explain that such non-
stationarity creates rapid changes in fraud patterns and conclude that
such issues cause significant scaling and complexity challenges in fraud
data due to the variability in fraud characteristics. Dal Pozzolo et al.
(2017) explore different characteristics of fraudulent transactions, in-
cluding consumer and merchant information. The authors define these
as demographic instances of consumer and merchant and financial
nature that describe a transaction. These instances are non-stationary,
given the vast variability of characteristics that cause fraud patterns
to occur rapidly, even in short periods. He, Leslie, and Grant (2024)
explore the nature of non-stationary characteristics in fraudulent activ-
ity on a time series basis. The authors propose a hybrid approach with
a classical time series and NN methodology applied alongside a fuzzy
clustering model. The non-stationary characteristics of fraud are only
defined in this study through the concept of a time series format. At
the same time, the authors do not consider how the different types of
fraud can influence rapidly changing patterns.
Lastly, there is a lack of exploration on how the proposed models
can be implemented on a real-time basis. Some works do not consider
how the proposed methods can be implemented in real-time (Srivastava
et al., 2008; Strelcenia & Prakoonwit, 2023). On the other hand,

by Abakarim et al. (2018). However, while the model is trained on
historical fraud data and then deployed for real-time classification, the
computational effort requirement is unclear. As the classification of live
transactions occurs in one main stage, there can be challenges with
computation time if large datasets are processed. The overall method of
historical data retrieval using structured query language pipelines can
increase complexity, with such systems being sensitive to changes in the
underlying data structures. These challenges can result in inefficiencies,
hence, the inability to detect fraud in real-time.
The challenges associated with the mentioned issues can detri-
mentally impact predictive accuracy, causing significant losses due to
fraudulent transactions or inconvenience to customers who are making
legitimate transactions classified as false positives. One method for
reducing false positive rates in fraud detection systems utilizes tree-
based rule generation ML algorithms, such as random forest, decision
tree, and gradient boosting (Vorobyev & Krivitskaya, 2022). While such
approaches can help reduce false positives, they do not address the
issues of false negative rates. This inflates fraud risk due to incor-
rectly classified fraudulent transactions. Nevertheless, these limitations
highlight the need for further research into implementing models in
real-time, assessing models appropriately for separation, imbalance,
and overall comprehensive performance and optimization evaluation.
The primary motivation of our work is to propose a new methodol-
ogy for real-time fraud detection that specifically addresses the critical
challenges identified in the literature. The focus is on the imbalance,
complete separation, and efficiency for real-time implementation is-
sues. Meanwhile, we address the rapid changes in fraud characteristics.
We utilize the fuzzy logistic regression framework of Charizanos et al.
(2023), which is shown to be robust against imbalance and separation
issues. We implement this approach in three stages: tuning, learning,
and online implementation. This framework offers essential benefits
in handling class imbalance and complete separation in the data,
enhanced interpretability through fuzzy membership values, flexibility
in decision boundaries, and applicability of the fuzzy logistic regression
framework on a real-time environment with large data (Charizanos
et al., 2023; Pourahmad, Ayatollahi, Taheri, & Agahi, 2011). Thus,
this study’s central objective is to develop a real-time transaction fraud
detection methodology that is not impacted by class imbalance and
separation problems, provides practitioners with low false positive and
high true positive detection rates, captures changing, non-stationary
fraud characteristics in the data, and can operate on smaller sam-
ple sizes without losing performance. This is achieved by using the
fuzzy classifier from Charizanos et al. (2023) and separately employing
tuning and learning stages using weighted historical data and then
implementing online fraud detection on the most recent transaction
data.
The practical contributions of our study are that (i) it provides a
highly precise and efficient solution for transaction fraud detection
using the fuzzy logistic regression framework on a real-time basis.
(ii) The proposed method handles the imbalance in the proportions
of fraudulent and legitimate transactions in the data without losing
accuracy. (iii) It is unaffected by the separation created due to data
characteristics. (iv) Our method successfully captures the changing
fraudster behavior and consumer characteristics to provide consistently
high precision. (v) Our framework is readily applicable in banking
practice as all the computer codes to implement the framework are
available at https://github.com/GZanos/FFL. From the methodological
viewpoint, the contributions of our study are that (i) we utilize a
probabilistic sampling technique to create the model’s training dataset
that allows us to capture changing fraud characteristics better and to
reduce the size of the required training dataset to increase the model’s
efficiency in online implementation. (ii) The use of fuzzy coefficients
and the fuzzification of the binary responses allow the model to account
for more uncertainty, resulting in better handling of class imbalance
and separation issues in fraud data.
Section 2 describes the data and proposed methodology in this
study. Section 3 depicts the application of the proposed online fraud
detection framework and compares it with other fraud detection ap-
proaches that use the same fraud dataset. Section 4 includes the study’s
conclusions and discussions about its limitations and future work.
2. Data and methodology
2.1. Real transaction fraud dataset
We utilize a European customer transaction dataset, which is a real
dataset of credit card transactions, including 284,807 transactions done
over two days, 492 of which are fraudulent. This real dataset is also
used or analyzed in many studies (Carcillo, Dal Pozzolo et al., 2018;
Carcillo, Le Borgne, Caelen and Bontempi, 2018; Carcillo et al., 2021;
Dal Pozzolo et al., 2017, 2014; Han, Zhu, Zhou, & Cai, 2022; Lebichot,
Le Borgne, He-Guelton, Oblé, & Bontempi, 2020). The dataset contains
29 predictors, including the amount of each transaction, named V29.
The total value of the transactions in the data is estimated to be $25.2
million US dollars, with the average transaction amount varying from
$0.01 USD to $25, 692 USD . The average amount for non-fraudulent
transactions is $88 USD, while for fraudulent transactions, it increases
to $122 USD. The dataset has 28 more characteristics, named from
V1 to V28, that are masked due to data sensitivity policy in financial
institutions.
As expected, the number of fraudulent transactions relative to the
size of the data indicates that the data is significantly imbalanced to-
wards normal transactions (𝑌 = 0 class) in the binary response variable,
given that only 0.17% of the transactions are actually fraudulent (𝑌 = 1
class).
We utilize scatter plots in Figs. 1, 2, and 3 across combinations
of predictors and the response variable to investigate for potential
complete separation in the data. Since the scatter plots are colored
based on the 𝑌 = 0 and 𝑌 = 1 classes, we can see if the classes in the
response variable are separated according to the pairs of independent
features in these scatter plots. Different combinations of predictors are
analyzed to assess which predictors perfectly separate the binary re-
sponse variable, Class, indicating whether the transaction is fraudulent
or not. Investigating the first set of predictor combinations, as shown in
Fig. 1, there is a distinct separation in the binary response variable for
V3–V4, V7–V8, V9–V10, and V11–V12 predictors. This is shown by the
positive class instances clustering away from the negative class. Fig. 2
shows more distinct clustering for V13–14, V15–16, and V17–18. Fig. 3
shows no obvious clustering for any of the final three combinations.
Thus, we can conclude that significant complete separation is present
for at least half of the combinations of predictors on the binary response
variable.

2.4.2. Handling non-stationary changes in fraud transaction data
Continuous changes in fraud characteristics govern the transaction
fraud environment. The types of transaction fraud can change suddenly
and frequently. This creates a highly sensitive environment towards
non-stationary changes and the necessity to handle such patterns in
the data. In our methodology, stratified random sampling ( Cochran,
1977; Kalton & Flores-Cervantes , 2003 ) is used to apply weights in
the transaction data using a timestamp component. In this way, we
assign a higher representation to the recent fraudulent transactions
than the older ones while keeping the level of randomness stable with
a probabilistic sampling technique ( Cochran, 1977).
The process to handle the data for the proposed fraud detection
framework is outlined in Fig. 8. The dataset of 𝑁 +𝑉 transactions that
will be used to train the fraud detection system is first divided into
training and validation portions of sizes 𝑁 and 𝑉 without changing
the order of transactions. Thus, a proportion of the transactions at the
last segment of the 𝑁 +𝑉 transactions is separated as the validation
portion. Random sampling must be avoided to preserve the changing
fraud and consumer characteristics while specifying the validation set.
The stratified random sampling is implemented on the training
portion of the whole dataset. The overall population of 𝑁 credit card
transactions in the training portion is first divided into two strata: 𝑆1
and 𝑆2, given a timestamp threshold 𝑡. This timestamp threshold is
user-defined and represents the desired timestamp that separates the
periods of recent and historical data. Random sampling is then used
to create two new samples 𝑠1 and 𝑠2 of sizes 𝑛1 and 𝑛2 from 𝑆1 and
𝑆2, respectively. 𝑛1 and𝑛2 are specified based on the characteristics of
the fraud dataset and the availability of computational resources. The
same rate of 𝑌 = 1 class is maintained in both 𝑠1 and 𝑠2. We analyze
the within-stratum variance, 𝑉𝑖,𝑖 = 1, 2, for both recent and historical
data using Eq. ( 2). A lower within-stratum variance indicates less
variability, hence a more homogeneous stratum that better represents
non-stationary changes in recent fraud characteristics.

2.4.3. Tuning and learning stages
The tuning stage aims to find the optimal parameter settings under
which the model showcases the best performance results for the fuzzy

limits for generating random coefficient values, degree of fuzziness 𝑚,

Users can also select different fuzzy thresholds for classification. The
model is implemented over a grid of different parameter settings. Per-
formance and optimization results are extracted for each combination
of the parameters in the grid, which allows for the assessment of how
the framework performs under different conditions. This performance
analysis allows for selecting the single optimal set of user-specified
parameters regarding the model’s performance. It also helps understand
the performance and optimization sensitivity of the model towards
important parameter settings, such as fuzzification and asymmetry, or
choosing different thresholds for classification.

2.4.4. Online implementation
In the online implementation stage, the new data come from the
fraud monitoring system after the tuning and learning stages. This is
represented by the ‘‘Online transaction database’’ in the flow chart in
Fig. 7. The new data must have the same features as in the historical
dataset used in the tuning and learning stages.
The fuzzy coefficient estimates ̄𝛽0 and ̄𝛽𝑖, extracted during the
learning stage, together with predictor information, are applied to each
new transaction coming live one by one using Eq. ( 1). This instantly
generates fuzzy probability estimates, which are classified using a fuzzy
threshold ̄ 𝜏= ( 𝑎1,𝑎 2,𝑎 3) to create predicted outcomes in the form
of ̄𝑌 = ( ̂ 𝑦1, ̂ 𝑦2, ̂ 𝑦3). Finally, a decision is made based on the shape of
each predicted TFN and vertex position. TFN predictions with a vertex
position of 1 indicate potentially a fraudulent transaction that must be
blocked. A vertex of 0 indicates a non-fraudulent transaction that can
be allowed to occur. If the new transaction is classified as fraudulent, a
notification is created for the relevant team to block the transaction and
notify the customer. The customer may receive a call from the relevant
banking department advising them of this activity and taking steps to
prevent it in future, which may involve the cancellation and re-issue
of credit card account numbers. If the transaction is not classified as
fraudulent, it is simply allowed to occur.

To examine the performance of our framework in an online en-

one on a test set of 𝑉 = 5000 transactions to mimic the processing of
5000 new transactions. These were initially extracted as the most recent
transactions list from the complete data to mimic an online environ-
ment. The time required to process 5000 transactions takes 8.4 min
in a simulated online environment with the computer mentioned in
Section 3.1. This corresponds to 0.1 s to process a new transaction in
an online environment.
The TFN coefficient estimates in Table 5 are plugged in Eq. (1)
along with the values of predictors for each transaction at a time.
Each transaction is evaluated in 0.0056 s on average, indicating that
a prediction for each transaction is efficiently generated on a real-
time basis. Each implementation of the model for each transaction
produces a fuzzy probability prediction. This fuzzy probability is then
classified using the method under Section 2.4.3, and a fuzzy prediction
is drawn in the form of a triangular fuzzy number. The vertex position
of each TFN prediction has the values of either 0 or 1. Following the
methodology defined in Section 2.4.4, a decision is made on whether
to block or allow each transaction.
After completing the processing of 5000 transactions, the confusion
matrix given Table 6 is obtained to assess classification performance
in the online environment. Out of 5000 transactions, we know that 10
transactions are fraudulent and 4990 are non-fraudulent. The model
correctly classifies 4962 out of 4990 non-fraudulent transactions and
10 out of 10 fraudulent transactions. At the same time, we also have

negative) and 17 transactions that are incorrectly identified as fraud-

the fuzzy logistic regression framework shows strong specificity and
sensitivity scores of 0.995 and 0.909, respectively. An F1 score of 0.526
indicates a good balance between precision and recall, while MCC at
0.809 suggests a balanced and better than random chance classifica-
tion performance. These are balanced results across all performance

3.4. Use of other ML methods in the proposed framework
We further implement five crisp ML methods to use as a benchmark
against the fuzzy logistic regression framework. These are Logistic
Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Naive Bayes, Artificial Neural
Network, and XG-Boost. All crisp ML methods were hyper-parameter-
tuned and trained on the same weighted stratum, also used for the
fuzzy logistic regression framework. Testing is also done using the same
online transactions, which were separated for testing purposes of the
fuzzy logistic framework as well. Following this approach, we simply
use a crisp ML model instead of the fuzzy logistic regression in our
fraud detection framework. Thus, the proposed framework and the set
of crisp ML methods are implemented on the same datasets to ensure
comparable results.
Table 7 shows the classification performance results of the online
implementation for the proposed framework and the benchmark ML
methods. As discussed, the online implementation of the fuzzy logistic
regression framework has high performance in detecting both fraudu-
lent and non-fraudulent transactions. On the other hand, all five crisp
ML methods show extremely high specificity and low sensitivity scores,
indicating that these ML methods performed well in identifying non-
fraudulent transactions but poorly in flagging fraudulent transactions.
Such performance is indicative of influence from strong imbalance
towards 𝑌 = 0 . F1 scores and MCC values remain relatively low
for these methods, providing further evidence that ML methods do
not work well, given the level of imbalance in the data. However,
given the higher sensitivity scores, fuzzy logistic regression handles this
imbalance well.
3.5. Comparison with other fraud detection methodologies
We compare the performance of our framework against other re-

One method for detecting fraud anomalies in transaction data is a
hybrid fuzzy approach that utilizes fuzzy clustering techniques with a
neural network (Fuzzy Clustering - ANN) (Behera & Panigrahi, 2015).
The fuzzy clustering part is used in the behavioral analysis phase,
with a neural network model in the learning stage. This methodology

training data is used to train the model, the validation data is used
to evaluate the model and hyperparameter tuning, and the test data
is used to evaluate the final online model. The analysis of results
using ROC curves indicates that fraudulent transactions have been
classified with 93.9% accuracy on the test dataset. Our framework
achieves a fraud detection rate of 90.9%, indicating a similar sen-
sitivity level in classifying fraudulent transactions. However, Behera
and Panigrahi (2015) do not mention the accuracy of non-fraudulent
transactions (i.e., negative class). If both specificity and sensitivity
were computed and analyzed, more targeted insights could be derived
to understand the impact of imbalance. Furthermore, our framework
handles the imbalance and complete separation issues without impact
on performance. This is shown by investigating additional performance
measures, such as F1 and MCC, achieving a more holistic understanding

4. Application with simulated transaction data
4.1. Results of tuning and learning stages
Parameter tuning is performed using the same settings in Sec-
tion 3.2. Table 2 of the Supplementary Material shows the tuning
and learning outcomes in terms of MSE, MAE, and RMSE. We see a
significant spread between the lowest and highest values achieved for
these optimization measures. However, we see consistently low scores
for all three optimization measures when analyzing the mean and
standard deviation values using CV. This showcases consistently strong
tuning outcomes with a small presence of outliers, which is evidenced
further in Fig. 4 of the Supplementary Material. All three box plots
indicate that a few scenarios produce outlier results for the optimization
measures; however, the overall distribution of results clusters closely
to the mean position. The selected optimal scenario has the following
settings. Fuzzification factor 𝑚 is set at 1.5. The asymmetry settings
are taken as 𝑙 = 1 and 𝑟 = 1 . The fuzzy classification threshold is
̄ 𝜏= (0.4, 0.5, 0.6). The fuzzification limits are 0 and 1.5. The coefficient
generation intervals for 𝛽0 and 𝛽𝑖 are set to [−2, 2].
Table 3 of the Supplementary Material presents the classification
performance outcomes for the optimal solution extracted from the
tuning and learning stage. We see similarly strong classification perfor-
mance with all three optimization measures, with specificity at 0.936
indicating accurate classification towards the negative class and sen-
sitivity of 0.992 indicating accurate classification towards the positive
class. The lack of significant variability between specificity and sensitiv-
ity strongly indicates that the imbalance problem does not impact the
model. Moreover, both F1 and MCC scores consistently indicate high
classification performance despite separation and imbalance issues in
the data.
Table 4 of the Supplementary Material shows TFN and crisp coeffi-
cient estimates obtained by the center of gravity method. There are two
more pieces of evidence that point towards the proposed methodology’s
strong classification outcomes. Firstly, there are no large values for any
of the coefficient estimates. Models impacted by complete separation
often produce extremely large coefficient estimates for the predictors
that cluster the binary responses. Secondly, none of the odds ratio
estimates (exponential values for the coefficient estimates) deviates
to infinity, which is a strong indicator of models not impacted by
separation or imbalance.

5. Conclusion and future work
In this study, we propose an innovative fraud detection method for
better precision by focusing on the issues of handling non-stationary
changes in fraud behavior, imbalance, and complete separation. To
handle these issues, we implement a fuzzy logistic regression approach
with tuning, learning, and online implementation stages.
Transaction fraud characteristics change rapidly, limiting predictive
effectiveness given the non-stationarity of the mechanisms creating
predictors and responses. The performance of the proposed solution
shows that it is not affected by sample size and number of Monte Carlo
replications. This is a key contribution and crucial benefit towards
efficiency, providing a control factor towards non-stationary changes
in the online implementation. This novelty also allows researchers to
work with smaller sample sizes, enhancing the capacity for experimen-
tation. In comparison, most methodologies in the literature focus on an
implementation using the whole dataset, which can be inefficient and
hence not applicable for real-time detection (Maes et al., 2002; Shen
et al., 2007; Srivastava et al., 2008).
The results from performance analysis show that the proposed
framework exhibits a high degree of accuracy in detecting both fraudu-
lent and non-fraudulent transactions. There is no indication of negative
influence on performance due to the existence of imbalance and com-
plete separation in the fraud data, with consistently strong performance
results on both tuning, learning, and online implementation stages.
The model correctly classifies 90.9% of fraudulent (positive class)
and 99.5% of non-fraudulent (negative class) transactions. We also
see a low rate of false positive (transactions incorrectly classified as
fraudulent) and false negative (transactions incorrectly classified as
non-fraudulent) decisions. On the other hand, several approaches in the
literature focus only on the predictive accuracy of positive classes (Be-
hera & Panigrahi, 2015; Shen et al., 2007). It is crucial to utilize
measures that assess both positive and negative classes, given that
a complete assessment of the impact of class imbalance can only be
achieved by reviewing both classes of the binary response variable.
Compared to the recent fraud detection approaches, the proposed
online fraud detection framework using fuzzy logistic regression out-
performs the fuzzy clustering algorithm of Behera and Panigrahi (2015)
on the predictive accuracy of fraudulent activity. At the same time,
a complete assessment shows substantial accuracy for non-fraudulent
transactions. While the results against the tree-based ML method of
Vorobyev and Krivitskaya (2022) are fairly comparable in terms of
granular false and true positive or negative rates, overall, the proposed
framework has better sensitivity performance and imbalance handling.
Moreover, Strelcenia and Prakoonwit (2023) shows strong accuracy
and F1 scores, which could be impacted by the complete separation
in the data. The separation problem would create over-fitting issues,
making the performance scores almost perfect. On the other hand,
the proposed fraud detection framework showcases strong performance
towards both imbalance and separation issues.
The practical implication of the proposed framework is three-fold.
Firstly, since its performance is not heavily dependent on the training
sample size and all the software codes are readily available, it can
easily be integrated into existing fraud monitoring systems in real-time.
Thus, financial institutions can implement the proposed framework on
their systems at a low cost. Secondly, the proposed framework cap-
tures the changing characteristics of both the fraudster and consumer
sides. This provides the institutions with a sustainable system that
needs periodic re-training at a frequency specified by the institution.
Thirdly, our framework is highly precise in detecting fraudulent and
non-fraudulent transactions. A higher performance in fraud detection
with our framework implies less financial losses for a financial in-
stitution when implemented. It also means less account exposure to
fraudsters when the financial institution fails to flag and stop the
activity. Better precision in not interfering with legitimate transactions
with our framework translates to higher consumer satisfaction and less
consumer interaction, leading to a better operational environment for
the institutions.
It should be noted that using unequally spaced time series data
due to random sampling can introduce bias if not carefully handled.
However, this is not an issue in our study, as this is handled by ensuring
that the ranking of the data, or the order of transactions, is maintained
and autocorrelation information is not utilized in the method.
A limitation of fraud detection studies is the unavailability of real
and non-anonymized transaction datasets due to regulations on pub-
lishing financial data. Therefore, most studies either use the publicly
available transaction fraud dataset or do not share their data.
Further research could focus on the methodology for cross-industry
application of fuzzy classifiers for fraud prediction across different
types of fraud. While we have mainly focused on transaction fraud,
